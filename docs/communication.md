# NotAPoll communication guide

From Matteo's answers, 1 Oct 2026. This guide sets what NotAPoll says and how it should feel. It applies to every post,
caption, slide, video, the launch page and the website. `docs/publishing.md` keeps the mechanics (formats, checks,
banned words, benchmark rules); where the two differ, this guide wins on voice and message.

## Why we exist

Election coverage is clickbait, and forecasts are either distrusted or trusted blindly. Polls record what people say;
they miss how voters actually react to events and to the world around them. NotAPoll is a live experiment: can
technology make forecasting independent, by modelling how groups of voters react to the news instead of only asking
them?

The deeper point is human. Elections are decided by social dynamics, not by single answers to a pollster. Modelling
those dynamics is sociological work, done with technology in the service of people.

## What people should feel

- **First second:** "These graphics are beautiful; I want to look at this."
- **After reading:** "This seems right, and it's interesting. I want to know more, and I want to see whether it works."
- **About the voter personas:** impressed and curious. They are technical, but they stand for real people's
  characteristics and reactions, built from real survey answers. Never cold, never surreal, never "bots".
- **About fairness:** a Republican and a Democrat should each come away thinking "this isn't attacking my side; it's
  trying to show what's actually happening".

The atmosphere: **a live experiment, reported like journalism.** A little science and technology, a little game
energy, never clickbait. References: the Political Integrity Index (trusted as fair, fighting for the right things),
Politico's publishing rhythm.

## Who we talk to

Voters who want to understand elections and how far to trust forecasts; people who love technology; journalists;
academics; campaigns; young people; Americans and Europeans; Republicans and Democrats alike.

## Voice

- **"We", a small independent lab.** Matteo is not visible: no face, no voice, no personal story. Scalia Studio appears
  only on the about page and in the bios if kept.
- **Inspiring to talk to:** curious, precise, ambitious, grounded. Like a researcher who loves the subject and explains
  it well over coffee.
- **Human first.** Every sentence should show we are studying people, not replacing them. Cut anything that sounds
  anti-human, mechanical or surreal. Technology is the tool; people are the subject.
- **No humour for now.** No clickbait, no hype words, no emojis.
- **Fair to every side.** We never attack a party, a candidate or pollsters. We show where and why our engine sees
  something different, and let the reader judge.

## Transparency

Transparency is the brand. Right or wrong, we show what the engine said and what happened.

- **Being wrong** is a technical result of a live experiment: "this part didn't work, here's what we learned". No
  drama, no excuses.
- **Uncertainty is a political variable.** Show it honestly (ranges, "wins 7 in 10"). Frame the goal as reducing
  uncertainty, not pretending it away.
- **The method lives on the website.** Posts don't explain the method every time. Each caption ends with a short,
  fixed disclaimer (to be written once, e.g. "Social simulation, not a poll. How it works: notapoll.org"). Some posts
  are about the method; most are about results.
- **Keep text on slides short.** Every word must earn its place.

## What we post

- **Normal days:** the progress of the experiment. Beautiful, clear, interesting: where races are heading and why.
- **Big news days:** a post that clearly signals importance and captures attention, without clickbait.
- **Where we disagree with the polls**, and why: the most interesting thing we can publish. Pre-announced, then
  scored.
- **Engine evidence posts:** "this news moved these voter groups by this much, so these races shifted". Only when the
  engine actually shows it.
- **Weekly, recurring:**
  - where our engine says the polls are wrong, and how last week's calls did;
  - the upset of the week: what changed most, and how it could affect the election.
- **Behind the engine (occasional):** how the engine is built; how we fine-tune models on real survey data; how we put
  people at the centre of the model. AI and model names may appear here, in plain words.
- **Shareable by design:** posts people screenshot and argue about because they're interesting, never because they're
  provocative.

## Website and posts

Posts keep people updated and make them curious. The website is for people who want to understand: the forecast, the
methods, every race. It should feel dynamic: click a state on the map, zoom into it, land on that race's page with
what the engine says and why.

## Look

Keep the current palette: white, cream, blue, red, purple, touches of grey, plus a little **yellow** for post-it style
highlights (a note, a flag, "what changed"). Keep the current post design, maps included. The hills logo and the serif
wordmark stay.

## Words

- **Say:** social simulation; voter personas (built from real survey answers); voter groups; live experiment;
  transparent; independent; simulated elections; prediction markets; "wins 7 in 10".
- **Never:** synthetic voters; bots; agents (in posts); betting; poll or survey for our outputs; AI-powered,
  revolutionary, game-changer; anything that makes people sound like data or the work sound anti-human.
- **Never mention** Hungary or Matteo's personal story.
- **Tagline:** "Democracy, rehearsed." (kept).

## About page, in one breath

What we want (independent, transparent forecasting that understands how people actually react), how we do it (voter
personas built from real survey answers, reacting to the daily news, played out 40,000 times), and that it's a live
experiment scored in public.

## Election night

Show up as an experiment reaching its result: what the engine said, what happened, what worked and what didn't. If it
worked, celebrate the experiment. If it didn't, the transparency is the story.
